I had been wanting to work with cherry for a while but somehow never really had, at least not on any meaningful project. We're blessed in the US with an abundant supply of domestic hardwoods — walnut, oak, maple, and cherry being the big four — and I'd worked extensively with the first three. Cherry was the holdout.

I'd also been gradually improving the usability of my house's small sun room. The room has knotty pine paneling, a brick fireplace, and an awkward corner that defeated every furniture arrangement I tried. Nothing fit well, and anything freestanding left dead space behind it. What the room needed was built-in seating — something that could wrap the corner and make use of every inch. So I decided to build it out of cherry.

## Design

The inspiration came from an unlikely place: the interior of a Concordia yawl. The Concordia 41 is arguably the most beautiful production sailboat ever built, and its interior joinery is famously considered. I'd spent some time aboard one and couldn't stop thinking about how the built-in settee berths worked — the proportions, the way the seating wraps around the cabin, the storage underneath. A sun room is not a sailboat, but the design problem is the same: make a small, irregular space comfortable and functional.

## The curvy bits

The bench arms are curved — not because curves are inherently better, but because the room geometry demanded it. A squared-off arm would have jutted into the walkway and made the whole thing feel like a wall.

I laminated the curves from thin strips of cherry over a form, the same technique I've used for [boat frames](/posts/finishing-frames-may23/). Thin strips are coated with epoxy, stacked on the form, and clamped until cured. The result holds its shape with no internal stress and follows whatever curve the form dictates.

## Case sides and drawer fronts

The case sides are frame-and-panel construction — cherry stiles and rails capturing a solid cherry panel, with breadboard ends to keep the panel flat as it moves with seasonal humidity changes.

The drawer fronts were cut directly out of the case side panels — one continuous piece of wood, sawn into sections. This means the grain flows unbroken from the case side across the drawer fronts, which is the kind of detail that's subtle until you notice it, and then you can't unsee it.

The trade-off is structural. Cutting the drawer fronts out of the panel leaves the remaining case side with sections of short grain — the wood fibers run across the narrow remaining sections rather than along them, making those spots fragile. These areas needed reinforcement from behind. It's the sort of compromise that comes with chasing a specific visual effect: worth it, but not free.

## Assembly and storage

Assembly is where the joinery either works or it doesn't. I used wedged through-tenons and drawbored pegs throughout — both are self-locking joints. Once the wedges are driven and the pegs set, the structure is under mechanical tension and locked permanently. There's no going back, but there's also nothing to fail. No glue to creep, no hardware to loosen.

## Cushions

I made the cushions from scratch, which I hadn't done before. The process: buy foam in two firmnesses, laminate them together (firm on the bottom, soft on top), wrap each block in dacron batting, then sew a cover and zip it over the foam. The dacron softens the edges and keeps the foam from grabbing at the fabric.

Cherry is not the easiest wood to work — it burns on the table saw, chatters under a router, and any sanding scratch you miss will announce itself the moment finish goes on. But the payoff is a surface that no other domestic hardwood can match. The chatoyance, the warmth of the color, the way it deepens with age. A year from now this bench will look better than it does today, and a decade from now better still. That's the deal with cherry — you build it, and then time finishes the job.
