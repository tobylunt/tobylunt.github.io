With the new mahogany sternpost installed, it was time to rehang the rudder. This turned out to involve more earthmoving than expected.

## Digging the hole

The rudder drops out the bottom of the boat. There's no other way — it slides down through the rudder port in the sternpost and exits below the hull. The problem is that the boat is sitting on blocks, and there isn't enough clearance underneath to pull the rudder clear. So we dug a hole.

<CaptionedImage
    src={img_b273_876e_4e9c_92e7_73c64f6a5768}
    alt="Hole dug beneath the stern of the boat with the rudder dropping down into it"
    caption="The hole. Not glamorous, but without it the rudder has nowhere to go. The rudder blade is visible dropping down through the rudder port."
/>

It's a surprisingly large hole — deep enough to get the full length of the rudder below the keel, and wide enough to maneuver it out at an angle. The kind of job where you start with a shovel thinking it'll take twenty minutes and an hour later you're still at it.

## Drilling the rudder port

The trickiest part of the whole operation was drilling the hole through the new sternpost for the rudder post to pass through. Get this wrong and the rudder hangs crooked, which means the boat won't steer straight. There's no fixing it after the fact — the hole has to be right the first time.

We used a jig clamped to the sternpost to guide the drill. Ideally you'd use a barefoot auger for a job like this — an auger bit without the pilot screw, so it doesn't pull itself through the wood and lets you control the feed rate. We didn't have one, so we worked carefully with what we had.

## A new copper strap

The rudder post is held against the sternpost at the midpoint by a strap — a piece of metal that wraps around the post and fastens to the sternpost to keep the rudder from wandering. The original strap was, to put it charitably, not up to the task.

<CaptionedImage
    src={img_e17a405d_871c_4262_8a48_1c258d015298_preview}
    alt="The original rudder post strap, a thin corroded piece of metal resembling plumber's tape"
    caption="The original strap. This is what was holding the rudder post in place — a piece of metal that looks like it came off a plumbing job. The corrosion and the crude hole pattern don't inspire confidence."
/>

We made a new one from copper plate. Copper is the right material here — it's compatible with the bronze rudder hardware, won't cause galvanic corrosion in salt water, and it's soft enough to form but stiff enough to hold.

## Hanging the rudder

With the new sternpost already in place, we could now hang the rudder. We found an off-the-shelf bronze gudgeon for the bottom bearing — one of those rare cases where a production fitting matched what we needed.
