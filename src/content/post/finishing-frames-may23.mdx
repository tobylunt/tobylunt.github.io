Frames are the ribs of a wooden boat. They run athwartships from the keel up to the covering boards, and every plank in the hull is fastened to them. If the backbone is the spine, the frames are the skeleton that gives the hull its shape and holds the skin together. When they fail, the boat starts to come apart — slowly at first, then with increasing urgency.

Ariadne's original frames were steam-bent white oak, installed by her Swedish builder in the early 1930s. They were copper riveted — no iron sickness to worry about — and after ninety years the wood itself was still in remarkably good shape. The problem was tension cracks. Nearly every frame had developed fractures along its outboard face, which is the classic failure mode for steam-bent frames: the wood is forced around a curve and stores tremendous internal stress, and over decades of wetting and drying cycles the outside of the bend eventually gives way. We [previously laminated](/posts/2019-05-01-remote-work/) a batch of replacement frames for the forward third of the boat, but the remaining stations still needed attention.

## The plan: steam bending

When we replaced the forward frames a few years ago, we laminated them — gluing up thin strips of white oak over a form with epoxy. Lamination produces a strong, stable frame with very little internal stress, but it's slow. Each blank needs to be templated, glued up on a custom form, cured overnight, and then beveled to fit. For twenty frames, that was fine. For the remaining stations, we wanted to try the original method: steam bending.

The obstacle we'd identified earlier was access. Steam bending is traditionally done from above — you drop the hot frame down through the open hull and lever it into position against the planking. On a boat with her covering boards still in place, there's no room for that.

Doug Hylan's article in WoodenBoat offered a solution: bend the frames from inside the hull, using a lever and bending strap that work from below the covering boards.

## Milling stock

The stock is quartersawn white oak, ripped to the correct cross section on the table saw. Quartersawn matters here — the grain orientation makes the wood more resistant to splitting when it's forced around a curve, and more dimensionally stable once it's in the boat.

## Testing

We built our own bending strap and leverage strongback based on the references above, along with a homemade steam box from HVAC duct and a wallpaper steamer. The whole apparatus cost less than a hundred dollars in parts.

The shop tests went beautifully. Frame blanks came out of the steam box pliable and cooperative, bent cleanly around our forms, and set without a single fracture. We were feeling confident.

## Best laid plans

Then we tried it in the boat. And it didn't work.

The problem wasn't the steam box or the bending strap — those performed fine. The problem was the confined space inside the hull. Bending a frame on an open table with full leverage and room to maneuver is one thing. Bending it in the cramped interior of a thirty-foot sailboat, working under the covering boards with limited swing, is something else entirely. We couldn't get the blanks around the tighter curves fast enough. By the time we had the strap positioned and the leverage right, the wood had cooled past the point of no return.

We ran through a discouraging amount of good white oak before accepting that this approach, however elegant in theory, wasn't going to work in our situation.

## Plan B: lamination in place

So we went back to what we knew worked — lamination — but adapted the technique for speed. Instead of building custom forms for each station, we laminated the frames in place: thin strips of white oak laid up directly against the planking inside the hull with thickened epoxy, then clamped from the outside with blocks screwed through the planking. Each frame was screwed down from the inside out to hold the lamination tight against the hull while the epoxy cured.

It's less romantic than steam bending, and Doug Hylan might not approve. But it works. Each laminated frame conforms perfectly to the hull shape, carries no internal stress, and is bonded directly to the planking. There's an argument that it's actually a better frame — it just takes longer.

With each frame installed, the boat gets noticeably stiffer. You can feel it when you push on the planking — where it used to flex and creak, it now feels solid. It's the kind of progress that doesn't photograph well but makes an enormous difference to the structure.

Framing a boat is not glamorous work, and this time around it came with a healthy serving of humility. We built a steam box, milled beautiful stock, ran flawless tests — and then the boat said no. The frames got done anyway, just not the way we planned. With her skeleton rebuilt, we can move on to planking repairs and start thinking seriously about when this boat touches water again.
