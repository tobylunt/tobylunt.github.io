Planking a hull is one of those jobs that sounds straightforward — you're just nailing boards to a frame — but the reality is that every plank is a unique three-dimensional puzzle. Each one has to be spiled to shape, beveled to match its neighbors, and often twisted or bent to follow the hull's compound curves. And in the tuck at the stern, where the planks converge sharply, the geometry gets genuinely wild.

## Spiling

Spiling is how you transfer the shape of the hull onto a flat plank. You can't just hold a board up to the hull and trace — the curves are three-dimensional and the plank stock is flat. Instead, you clamp a thin spiling batten to the frames where the plank will go, then use dividers to transfer a series of reference points from the hull's edge onto the batten. Back at the bench, you reverse the process to draw the plank's true shape onto the stock.

We found that spiling alone wasn't accurate enough for the hood ends — where the planks land on the stem or sternpost. The geometry is too tight and the tolerances too small. So we pre-templated the hood ends separately: a full-size template cut from thin plywood, then hot-glued to the end of the spiling board (made from door skin). This gave us the exact shape at the ends where it mattered most.

## Rolling bevels

The planks don't meet their neighbors at a right angle — the bevel changes continuously along the length of the plank as the hull curvature shifts from station to station. This is a rolling bevel, and getting it right is what makes the seams between planks tight.

We measured the bevel at each frame station using a bevel gauge, then wrote the readings directly on the plank. These readings become a roadmap: at station 22 the bevel is one angle, at station 30 it's another, and everything in between has to transition smoothly.

<CaptionedImage
    src={img_2281}
    alt="Scrap plywood with station-by-station bevel readings written in marker"
    caption="Station-by-station bevel readings written on scrap plywood and kept at the planing station for reference. Each number is a frame station, each angle mark shows the bevel to cut at that point. This is the roadmap for shaping."
/>

<CaptionedImage
    src={img_fc66c1e_87a9_4663_82d2_69291d764c0d_preview}
    alt="Squaring up a plank seam with a rabbet plane on the hull"
    caption="Squaring up a plank seam with a rabbet plane. When planking top-down, the bottom edge of every plank is square — the rolling bevels are only on the top edge. This plank was the hardest and weirdest one, so we had to add this extra step to get a clean seam."
/>

The bevels are cut by hand with planes on a two-vise workbench — the plank is clamped on edge and you work the bevel in progressively, checking against your readings as you go.

## Matching floor timber camber

The inside face of each plank has to match the outside camber of the floor timbers it lands on. If the plank is flat on the inside and the floor timber is curved, you get a gap — the plank only contacts the timber at the edges, and the fasteners can't pull it tight. So you hollow the inside face of the plank to match.

    src={img_c1fb44c_aa50_4611_b882_e300d86bf9b5_preview}

    caption="A cross-section template taken at one frame station, labeled 'P38' with 'Floor' and 'Garboard' marked. The curves show the profile the plank's inside face needs to match — the concavity of the floor timber and the transition to the garboard."

## Fitting planks

Getting a plank to its final fit is an iterative process: hang it on the hull, check where it bears and where it gaps, take it down, adjust, repeat. Wedges are essential — they force the plank tight against its neighbor and hold it in position while you assess the fit. Without them you can't see where the problems are.

## The swell test

A carvel-planked hull relies on the wood swelling when it hits the water to close up the seams between planks. But how much will it swell? And how tight do the seams need to be on land so they close up properly in the water without becoming too tight and buckling?

We built a test rig — a small section of planking with controlled gaps of different widths — and sank it in a buddy's pond to find out.

## The tuck

The stern is where planking gets hard. The planks converge sharply as they wrap around the hull's tuck — the concave area under the counter where the shape transitions from the broad midship sections to the narrow sternpost. The planks fan together, the bevels change dramatically, and some planks need serious twist to follow the hull.

<CaptionedImage
    src={img_2284}
    alt="New planks converging sharply at the stern tuck"
    caption="The planks converging at the tuck. Each plank fans in toward the sternpost, and the angles between them change rapidly. This is where the geometry gets serious."
/>

For one plank right in the tuck, we had to cut twist directly into the stock — the plank needed to change its plane of rotation along its length. We cut the twist on the bandsaw, then steamed the plank to twist it even further for installation.
