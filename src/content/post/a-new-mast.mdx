Ariadne is a 22 Square Metre — a Swedish class of racing yacht designed in the 1920s that most people outside Scandinavia have never heard of. The boats are lovely: long overhangs, a low-slung rig, and proportions that make them look fast standing still. They were built to a box rule that governed sail area and a handful of hull dimensions but left the builder considerable freedom in how to get there, which means no two are quite alike.

When we began Ariadne's restoration, we assumed the mast would be one of the easier chapters. The spar was original — a hollow spruce stick that had been aloft for the better part of a century. It had some cracks, some old repairs, and a lot of character. Our plan was to stabilize the damage, replace the worst sections, and put it back up. That turned out to be optimistic.

## The old spar

The first step was a thorough inspection. We pulled the mast out of storage, set it on sawhorses, and started looking. Up close, it was worse than we remembered.

## Measuring up

Before we could build a replacement, we needed to know exactly what we were replacing. We spent several sessions recording every dimension of the old spar — cross-sections at every station, locations of every fitting and feature, the taper profile, the flat for the sail track. All of it went into a notebook.

## Engineering

With the old mast thoroughly documented, we brought in help. We hired James, a marine engineer, to work with us on scantlings and design for the new spar. The 22 Square Metre class has a box rule that specifies minimum mast diameters, and one of the first things James discovered was that the old mast — the one that had been racing and cruising for ninety years — didn't actually meet the rule.

One of the most consequential design decisions was eliminating the jumper strut. The old mast had a jumper — a short strut near the masthead with diamond stays to stiffen the upper spar. But the jumper wasn't original to the Swedish build. It was added at some point, probably to compensate for the mast's deterioration. James helped us design a new section that was stiff enough on its own, eliminating the jumper and all its associated windage, weight, and complication.

## Built like a barrel

A mast looks like a long stick, but it's actually built like a barrel. The new spar is hollow — eight Sitka spruce staves glued up around a central cavity, like coopering a cask. Sitka spruce is the traditional choice for spars: straight-grained, light, strong, and available in clear lengths. The staves taper in both width and thickness along their forty-foot run, so the finished spar has the correct cross-section at every point from heel to masthead.

No single piece of lumber stretches the full length, so each stave is assembled from two or three shorter pieces joined with scarf joints — long, angled glue joints that are effectively invisible once faired and stronger than the surrounding wood.

## Test run

Before committing to the full-length glue-up, we built a test section to answer a few questions. We wanted to try birdsmouth construction — where the staves have mating V-grooves along their edges instead of flat joints — and we wanted to see what would happen if we glued in a pre-bend at the mast tip. Would the bend spring back after the clamps came off? Would the curve introduce voids between the staves? The test piece also let us experiment with clamping methods.

## Internals

From the outside, a mast is a smooth taper. Inside, it's a complicated piece of plumbing. The halyards run internally — in through sheave boxes at the masthead, down through the hollow core, and out through exit slots lower on the spar. Internal halyards are cleaner than external ones (no slapping against the mast at anchor), but they add a layer of complexity to the build.

The real challenge is the blocking. At every point where the rigging applies a load — shroud tangs, forestay, backstay — there needs to be solid structure inside the mast to resist the compression. We used G10 fiberglass tube for many of these reinforcement points. G10 is strong, dimensionally stable, and bonds well with epoxy. But it has one significant drawback: the cut edges expose glass fibers, and if a halyard runs across those fibers, the glass threads work into the rope and destroy it. So every G10 element has to be positioned and faired so that the halyards run free without ever touching it. Internal routing becomes a puzzle: strong enough to support the rig loads, smooth enough that a running line doesn't know the blocking is there.

## Shaping

With the mast closed up, it's still a rough octagonal blank. Shaping is mostly hand tools — a drawknife takes the corners off the octagon to make it sixteen-sided, then planes and longboards fair it into a smooth ellipse. The taper runs continuously from butt to tip, which means the shaping has to blend along the full forty-foot length. There's no template you can hold up and check — you go by eye, by feel, and by running your hand along the spar.

## Hardware and rigging

For the standing rigging, we made a decision early on: synthetic fiber instead of wire. Synthetic rigging — typically Dyneema or similar high-modulus polyethylene — is dramatically lighter than stainless steel wire, doesn't corrode, and can be spliced by hand without a swaging machine. We had experience building and splicing our own rigging for International 14 skiffs, which are small high-performance racing dinghies where every gram aloft matters. The I14 world has been running synthetic rigging for years, and we'd built enough confidence in the material to bring it to Ariadne.

The one concern with synthetic rigging is pre-stretch. When you first tension a new set of shrouds, the splices settle under load — the fibers in the splice tails bed into each other and the whole assembly elongates slightly. It looks like creep, but it's not the fiber stretching — it's the joints finding their final geometry. If you don't account for this by pre-stretching the rigging before final installation, you'll be chasing your tune for weeks as the splices settle. We'll cover the shroud fabrication and pre-stretch process in a later post.

We went all bronze for the mast hardware — tangs, bands, bolts, sheave pins. Bronze is heavier than stainless but it doesn't corrode in contact with salt water the way stainless can when it's deprived of oxygen (crevice corrosion is what kills stainless on boats). For components where weight matters most, we used composite — composite sheaves for the halyard blocks and composite tangs for the shroud terminations. The composite pieces save weight aloft where it counts the most.

Working with bronze sheet turned out to be its own education. We needed 90-degree bends for the spreader brackets and dogleg bends for the shroud tangs. We wanted silicon bronze — the gold standard for marine hardware — but couldn't find it in half-hard or annealed temper. What we could get was phosphor bronze, which is harder and springier. The problem is that phosphor bronze in full-hard temper doesn't want to take a sharp bend without cracking. The solution is annealing: heating the metal to cherry red and letting it cool slowly, which softens the grain structure and makes it workable. But you have to anneal before every major bend, because the metal work-hardens as you form it. Miss a step and it lets you know.

## The finished spar

The new mast is Sitka spruce, hollow octagonal construction, and weighs less than a hundred pounds — lighter than the old spar, stiffer where it needs to be, and built to the class rule that the original never actually met. The jumper strut is gone. The rig is cleaner and simpler.
