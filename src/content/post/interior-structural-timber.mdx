Rebuilding a boat's backbone and planking gets most of the attention, but there's a whole category of structural timber that lives inside the hull and rarely gets photographed: frames, hanging knees, and floor timbers. These are the pieces that tie everything together — planking to backbone, deck beams to hull sides, cockpit sole to the structure below. None of them are visible in the finished boat, and all of them have to be right.

This post covers the interior structural work in Ariadne's cockpit area: a new set of laminated frames, hanging knees to support the deck beams, and cockpit floor timbers that had to be fitted around keel bolts and existing structure with their tops dead co-planar to carry the cockpit sole.

## Laminated frames

We'd [laminated frames before](/posts/2019-05-01-remote-work/) for the forward sections, so the process was familiar. Thin strips of white oak are glued up over a form with epoxy, clamped until cured, then removed and beveled to fit. The form is the critical piece — it defines the hull shape at each station, and every frame gets its own.

## Picking up bevels

A frame can't just be bent to the right curve — its outside face also has to be beveled so it sits flush against the inside of the planking. The hull is a compound curve, so the bevel angle changes continuously along the length of each frame. Getting this wrong means a frame that only contacts the planking at a few high points instead of bearing evenly along its full length.

## Hanging knees

Hanging knees are the brackets that connect the deck beams to the hull sides. They transfer loads between the deck and the hull structure — everything from the weight of someone standing on deck to the shock of a wave breaking over the bow. A knee that's too small or poorly fitted is a structural weak point; one that's right is invisible.

## Cockpit floor timbers

Floor timbers run athwartships across the top of the keel, tying the two halves of the hull together at the garboards. In the cockpit, they also serve as the structural support for the cockpit sole — which means their top faces have to be perfectly co-planar. Any error shows up as a rocking or uneven sole, and there's no way to cheat it after the fact.

The complication is that each floor timber has to be fitted around the keel bolts and their welded-on nuts that protrude through the top of the keel. Some floors are built in halves and glued up around the bolts with G-flex epoxy, with horizontal channels cut to clear the nuts. Every one is a custom piece: scribed to the hull shape on the bottom, notched around obstructions, beveled on the faces that bear against the planking, and leveled across the top.

None of this work will be visible when the boat is finished. The floor timbers will be hidden under the cockpit sole, the knees deep under the cabin house, the frames behind joinery. But it's the structure that everything else depends on — the cockpit sole can only be flat if the floor timbers are true, the deck can only stay attached if the knees are sound, and the planking can only hold its shape if the frames are there to hold it. The unglamorous work is the important work.
